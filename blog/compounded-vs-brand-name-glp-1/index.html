    Compounded vs. Brand-Name GLP-1s: What Patients Should Know

    Oakwell MD care team · September 27, 2026 · 6 min read

    Compounded semaglutide and tirzepatide became widely known during the national shortages of 2022 through 2024, when pharmacies were allowed to make copies of drugs on the FDA shortage list. The FDA has since taken both drugs off the shortage list, and that shift tightened what compounders can do. Compounding still exists. Here is a plain explanation of what that means for patients.

    What brand-name means

    Wegovy and Ozempic (semaglutide) and Zepbound and Mounjaro (tirzepatide) are made by their manufacturers and approved by the FDA. Wegovy and Zepbound carry the weight management approval, and Zepbound has an added approval for obstructive sleep apnea in adults with obesity. Ozempic and Mounjaro are approved for type 2 diabetes. Weight loss use of those two is off-label. Every batch goes through the same FDA-reviewed manufacturing process.

    Insurance coverage for brand drugs varies a lot by plan. Prior authorization is the usual hurdle, and insurers often want a recorded BMI plus notes on earlier weight loss efforts. Medicare rules on weight loss coverage have been changing. Manufacturer savings programs and direct-to-patient pharmacies exist for people paying cash, and the details change often.

    What compounded means

    A compounded medication is prepared by a licensed pharmacy for an individual patient based on a prescription. Compounded semaglutide and tirzepatide are not FDA-approved, which means the FDA has not reviewed that specific product for safety or effectiveness before it reaches you. 503A pharmacies compound for individual prescriptions under state board oversight, while 503B outsourcing facilities register with the FDA and follow federal manufacturing standards. Ask which kind your pharmacy is.

    Each 503A pharmacy makes each prescription for a named patient. Outsourcing facilities registered under 503B make bigger batches under current good manufacturing practice, the federal standard drug manufacturers meet. Both can be legitimate. What separates them is how much testing and inspection each one gets.

    Since the shortages ended, federal rules limit compounders from making what are essentially copies of an approved drug. A compounded version generally needs a documented clinical reason, such as a dose the brand does not offer or an allergy to an ingredient in the brand product. Some compounded products add vitamin B12 or glycine. There is no good evidence those extras change results.

    Real differences patients notice

    • Form: brand drugs come in prefilled pens, while compounded versions usually come in a vial you draw up with a syringe.
    • Dosing units: compounded vials are labeled in mg per mL, so your dose may be written in units on an insulin syringe.
    • Storage: most compounded vials need refrigeration and have a beyond-use date printed on the label.
    • Coverage: insurance may cover brand drugs for some patients, and compounded products are rarely covered.

    The dosing math is where errors happen. A vial of 2.5 mg per mL means 0.25 mg is 10 units on a 100-unit insulin syringe, and mixing up milliliters and units can lead to a tenfold overdose. The FDA has received reports of exactly that. Have your provider or pharmacist walk you through the first draw.

    Storage rules differ too. Brand pens can usually sit at room temperature for a limited time once in use, but a compounded vial generally lives in the fridge, between 36 and 46 degrees Fahrenheit. Check the beyond-use date on the label, and do not use the vial past it even if liquid remains. Cloudy liquid or floating particles mean you should call the pharmacy.

    How to judge a compounded source

    Quality varies between pharmacies. The FDA has warned about counterfeit semaglutide and products sold online labeled for research use only, which are not meant for people. A legitimate prescription goes through a licensed provider and a state-licensed pharmacy that you can look up on your state board of pharmacy website. Salt forms such as semaglutide sodium or acetate are not the same as the base drug, and the FDA has said it is not aware of a lawful basis for compounding with them.

    Red flags are easy to spot once you know them. Be wary of any seller that skips the medical questionnaire, ships without a prescription, or will not name the pharmacy. A legitimate vial label lists the patient name, the prescriber, the concentration and a beyond-use date. Missing any of those is a reason to stop.
